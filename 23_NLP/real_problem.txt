PROBLEM A: "My sentiment classifier scores 95% accuracy on the test set
but fails badly on tweets from today."
WHY IT HAPPENS
  1. Distribution shift: the training data was collected 2 years ago;
     slang, emojis, and topics changed (covariate shift on words).
  2. The model latched onto spurious cues: e.g. brand names, hashtags,
     or the word "movie" correlating with positive reviews in the
     training corpus - those cues no longer predict anything.
  3. Accuracy is misleading when classes are imbalanced: 95% may be
     "predict positive for everything".
HOW TO SOLVE
  1. Report precision/recall/F1 per class, not just accuracy.
  2. Build a small labeled sample of CURRENT text; measure the accuracy
     drop; use it to drive re-annotation.
  3. Check what the model actually uses: SHAP/LIME on a handful of
     examples will expose the spurious cues (see 14_MODEL_EVALUATION).
  4. If shift is inevitable, use a smaller vocabulary / robust features
     (character n-grams generalize across slang better than word ids),
     or schedule continuous re-training on new data.

PROBLEM B: "Named-entity extraction misses entities in my legal
documents even though the same model works on news text."
WHY: domain mismatch - the model's pretraining and fine-tuning data
(usually news/web) rarely contains legal phrasing ("the aforementioned
Party of the First Part"), long nested clauses, or unusual entity
types (clause numbers, statute references). Word-piece tokenization
splits legal terms so the model never sees them whole.
HOW TO SOLVE
  1. Build a domain dictionary of the terms you care about; use
     dictionary/rule-based matching as a FIRST pass and merge its hits
     with the ML model's hits.
  2. Label 200-500 domain sentences and fine-tune (or adapt) the NER
     model; NER is cheap to adapt because you only retrain the tagger
     head.
  3. Check tokenization: if "§ 17200" becomes 4 tokens, the model
     cannot learn it as one entity - add the phrase to the vocab or use
     a whitespace pre-tokenizer override for identifiers.

PROBLEM C: "Summarization produces fluent summaries that omit the main
conclusion of the article."
WHY: extractive biases of the model: it copies the LEAD paragraph
(news-trained summarizers assume the first paragraph carries the
answer) and the conclusion is compressed away by length limits.
HOW TO SOLVE
  1. Score summaries against the source with an abstractive metric
     (factual consistency, e.g. entailment-based NLI score) in addition
     to ROUGE - ROUGE rewards copied text, which is exactly the bias
     you are fighting.
  2. If the conclusion always matters, restructure the input so the
     conclusion is not at the end (move it second, after the lead) or
     use guided summarization with a pointer to the required section.
  3. Raise the max output length and instruct the model to output
     "Key conclusions" as a mandatory section.

PROBLEM D (debugging): "TF-IDF vectorizer exploded: 2M features from a
1M-document corpus; training now takes an hour."
WHY: default settings keep every token that appears in >= 2 documents;
with a large corpus, rare tokens dominate the vocabulary, most are
noise for a downstream classifier.
HOW TO SOLVE
  1. Add max_features=50_000 (or 100k), min_df=5, max_df=0.8 (drop
     ultra-common stop-like words), and use sublinear_tf=True.
  2. Consider hashing (HashingVectorizer, no vocab dict, streaming).
  3. Run a quick fit on a 10% sample to size the vocabulary before the
     full run.
