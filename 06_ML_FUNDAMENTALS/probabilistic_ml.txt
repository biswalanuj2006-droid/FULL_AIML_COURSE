================================================================================
LESSON: PROBABILISTIC ML (beyond point estimates)
================================================================================
Companion: 10_REGRESSION/bayesian_and_gp_regression.txt derives Bayesian
linear regression and Gaussian processes in full. This lesson covers the
rest of the probabilistic toolbox: generative vs discriminative models,
Bayesian networks, hidden Markov models, and uncertainty estimation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE PROBABILISTIC VIEW OF LEARNING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A supervised model is a conditional distribution p(y|x, w). Three ways to
choose w:
  MLE      : w = argmax_w  p(D|w)             (fit the data only)
  MAP      : w = argmax_w  p(w) * p(D|w)      (MLE + prior = ridge-like
                                               shrinkage, see GP lesson)
  Bayesian : keep the FULL posterior p(w|D) ∝ p(w) p(D|w), then predict
             p(y|x, D) = integral p(y|x, w) p(w|D) dw
The Bayesian answer to "what if I'm not sure about w?" is to average over
w, which produces calibrated uncertainty. The cost: the integral is
intractable except for conjugate pairs - hence approximations.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. GENERATIVE vs DISCRIMINATIVE MODELS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Discriminative: model p(y|x) directly (logistic regression, neural
  nets, trees). Optimized for the decision you care about; needs less
  data to separate classes; cannot generate data; no principled way to
  handle missing inputs.
  Generative: model p(x, y) = p(y) * p(x|y) (naive Bayes, GMMs, HMMs,
  diffusion models). You can sample new (x, y); you get p(x) for free
  (anomaly detection!); handle missing features by marginalizing; need
  more data because you model the harder object p(x|y).
RULE OF THUMB: discriminative for pure prediction with enough labels;
generative when you need p(x) (novelty), missingness, small-data priors,
or generation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. BAYESIAN NETWORKS (directed graphical models)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A DAG where each node is a random variable and an edge X -> Y means Y
depends directly on X. The joint factorizes:
  p(X1..Xn) = product_i p(Xi | parents(Xi))
This factorization is the point: it turns an exponential joint into a
product of small conditional tables, and it encodes ASSUMPTIONS
(conditional independencies) you can read off the graph:
  X independent of Y given Z if every path between X and Y is
  "blocked" by Z (d-separation).
APPLICATION: expert-model + data: encode causal/domain structure as the
graph, learn the conditional tables from data, then answer queries
p(Query | Evidence) by sum-product (exact) or sampling (approximate).
TRAP: "Bayesian network" does not imply causal - a correlation graph
learned from data (PC algorithm) is not a causal model without
intervention assumptions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. HIDDEN MARKOV MODELS (HMMs)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A generative model for sequences: at each time t there is a hidden state
z_t (categorical) evolving by transition matrix A, and an observation
x_t drawn from an emission p(x_t | z_t) (discrete or Gaussian). Joint:
  p(z_1..z_T, x_1..x_T) = p(z_1) * prod_t p(z_t|z_{t-1}) p(x_t|z_t)
The THREE canonical problems (know these by heart):
  1. Likelihood p(x_1..x_T | A, emission): FORWARD algorithm - dynamic
     programming over states, O(T * K^2). The forward variable
     alpha_t(i) = p(x_1..x_t, z_t = i).
  2. Decoding (most likely state path): VITERBI - same DP but with max
     instead of sum; recover z*_1..z*_T.
  3. Learning A and emissions from unlabeled sequences: BAUM-WELCH
     (EM). E-step: forward-backward to get soft counts; M-step:
     renormalize counts into A and emission parameters.
USES: part-of-speech tagging (classic), gene finding, speech, and as the
simplest "latent-state time-series" model to understand before state-
space models and (conceptually) RNNs. REMEMBER: HMM assumes the hidden
state is memoryless - the future depends on the past only through z_t.
That is its main limitation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. UNCERTAINTY ESTIMATION FOR REAL MODELS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two kinds of uncertainty (know the distinction):
  Aleatoric: irreducible noise in the data (a fair coin). Cannot be
  reduced with more data; must be MODELED (predict variance, not just
  mean).
  Epistemic: uncertainty about the model because of finite data.
  Reducible with more data; this is what Bayesian inference captures.
PRACTICAL TOOLS (when full Bayesian is intractable):
  1. Deep ensembles: train 5-10 models with different seeds; treat the
     spread of predictions as epistemic uncertainty. Cheap, and often
     better calibrated than fancier methods.
  2. Monte-Carlo dropout: keep dropout on at test time; run N forward
     passes; mean = prediction, variance = uncertainty. Approximates a
     Bayesian average over weights.
  3. Temperature scaling / isotonic calibration: fix the CALIBRATION of
     a single model's probabilities post hoc (train on validation logits
     to map them to true frequencies). Fast, and usually sufficient
     when you only need "is 0.9 really 0.9?".
  4. Laplace approximation: Gaussian around the MAP point - a cheap
     Bayesian-ish posterior.
WHEN UNCERTAINTY MATTERS (real systems):
  - Reject low-confidence predictions for human review (medical,
    fraud).
  - Out-of-distribution detection: epistemic uncertainty should spike
    for OOD inputs (in practice deep models are often overconfident -
    see 46_PRODUCTION_AI/ml_security.txt and OOD robustness).
  - Decision making under risk: expected-cost decisions need the full
    distribution, not the point estimate.
VERIFICATION: calibration curve (bin predictions by confidence, plot
accuracy within each bin; a calibrated model follows the diagonal) and
Brier score.

   with beta prior; show the posterior is beta and the predictive is
   the posterior mean.
2. (Reasoning) Why does the Bayesian predictive integral protect
   against overconfidence far from the data, while MLE does not?
3. (Concept) One sentence each: discriminative vs generative. When does
   a generative model let you do anomaly detection for free?

   joint factorization and list which independencies hold given D.
5. (Math) HMM forward algorithm: write the recursion for alpha_t(i) and
   say why it is O(T K^2) not O(T K^T).
6. (Code) Implement Viterbi decoding for a two-state HMM given A,
   emission probabilities, and an observation sequence.
7. (Reasoning) HMM vs RNN/LSTM: what assumption does the HMM make that
   RNNs drop, and what price do RNNs pay for dropping it?
8. (Design) A credit model returns 0.9 for a loan. The bank needs to
   know when 0.9 means "confident approve" vs "the model has never seen
   this applicant profile". Which uncertainty do they need, and which
   tool from section 5 do you deploy first?
9. (Code) Implement MC-dropout uncertainty on a small MLP: predict with
   dropout on 50 times, return mean + std.
10. (Concept) Aleatoric vs epistemic: a self-driving car sees fog
    (sensor noise) vs a road type never in training. Classify both.
11. (Debugging) Your calibrated model is overconfident again after
    deployment. List 3 causes and which tool re-diagnoses them.
12. (Open-ended) OPEN PROBLEM: reliable out-of-distribution uncertainty
    for deep nets remains unsolved - modern models are overconfident on
    shifted inputs. Design an evaluation that would convince you a new
    method actually solved it.
